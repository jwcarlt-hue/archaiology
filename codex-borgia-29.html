The Death of the Evening Star

Plate 29 of the Codex Borgia opens a unique eighteen-page narrative (pages 29\u201346) that Susan Milbrath reads as a Venus almanac set within the festival year. The page stages a single astronomical moment \u2014 ELast, the last visibility of Venus as the Evening Star \u2014 as the death of Quetzalcoatl in the fire, from whose ashes the Morning Star will rise. Tap a marker to explore the imagery.

Provenance
The Codex Borgia is a Central Mexican divinatory manuscript, not a Maya book. Painted before the Spanish conquest in the Puebla-Tlaxcala Valley \u2014 most likely by Tlaxcaltec or Cholultec scribes of the \u201cMixteca-Puebla\u201d tradition \u2014 its imagery is Nahua and its gods Aztec. It is placed here alongside the Maya material as part of a shared Mesoamerican sky, but its cultural attribution is distinct.

Reading the page

After Milbrath 1989, 2013 · synopsis after Byland 1993
The Venus almanac
Milbrath argues that Borgia 29\u201346 tracks the synodic cycle of Venus across an eighteen-month festival calendar, each festival on its own page, read top to bottom. The sequence begins here with the disappearance of the Evening Star and moves toward its rebirth as the Morning Star.
A candidate date
Milbrath's seasonal anchoring places this festival around the winter solstice. One proposed correlation falls in the dry season of 1495\u20131496 (Julian), with Venus reaching its last evening visibility in early January \u2014 though the exact historical year the codex encodes remains debated.
The myth behind the image
The Anales de Cuauhtitl\u00e1n tell how Quetzalcoatl immolated himself and became the Morning Star. The page draws on that myth to explain an astronomical fact: the planet that dies in the west as the Evening Star is the same one that rises in the east as the Morning Star.
A contested manuscript
No single reading of Borgia 29\u201346 commands consensus. Others \u2014 Seler, Nowotny, Anders & Jansen, Boone \u2014 have read the pages as ritual, as a journey through named places, or as a creation narrative. Milbrath's astronomical reading is one influential interpretation among several, not a settled decipherment.
On certainty
Every marker on this plate carries an interpretation, not a caption the codex supplies for itself. The Codex Borgia has no glosses; its meaning is reconstructed from comparative iconography, colonial texts, and \u2014 in Milbrath's case \u2014 retrocalculated astronomy. The identifications here follow her published work and should be read as a well-argued scholarly proposal that other specialists dispute.
