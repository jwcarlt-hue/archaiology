The Double Frame of Izcalli

Plate 31 is unique in the Venus almanac: it is the only page with two festival periods, drawn as two separate framed scenes. The upper and lower frames carry the twenty days of Izcalli and the five nemontemi — the "empty" days that closed the year. On Susan Milbrath's reading the page falls in the cold, dry February of 1496, when Venus still rose low in the dawn and the plants of the fields lay dormant. Tap a marker to explore the imagery.

Provenance
The Codex Borgia is a Central Mexican divinatory manuscript. Painted before the Spanish conquest in the Puebla-Tlaxcala Valley — most likely by Tlaxcaltec or Cholultec scribes of the "Mixteca-Puebla" tradition — its imagery is Nahua and its gods Aztec.

Two frames, two festivals.

Alone in the almanac, this page splits into an upper and a lower scene — the twenty days of Izcalli and the five closing nemontemi. Around them run bathing rites, dormant plants, circled day-signs for reckoning festivals across four years, and skeletal goddesses who may carry a lunar eclipse. Reading the page

After Milbrath 1989, 2013 · synopsis after Byland 1993
Two festivals, one page
This is the only page in the almanac with two festival periods, and it shows them in two separate frames: the twenty-day veintena of Izcalli, and the five nemontemi — the unlucky "empty" days that closed the 365-day year before the next cycle began. Milbrath dates the span to 26 January–19 February 1496 (Julian).
The dead season
The imagery is wintry. Skeletal vegetation goddesses and the death aspects of maize, grass (malinalli) and maguey show these plants lying dormant in the cold, dry February. The ritual bathing on the page refers to Izcalli itself, a festival that involved bathing and the warming of the fields toward the coming year.
A possible lunar eclipse
Milbrath suggests the blindfolded skeletal goddesses may depict the lunar eclipse visible in central Mexico on 30 January 1496, linking them to the Cihuateteo — the dangerous deified women who died in childbirth and haunted the crossroads. Venus, meanwhile, still rises in the east but sits low in the dawn sky.
A contested manuscript
No single reading of Borgia 29–46 commands consensus. Others — Seler, Nowotny, Anders & Jansen, Boone — have read the pages as ritual, as a journey through named places, or as a creation narrative. Milbrath's astronomical-seasonal reading is one influential interpretation among several, not a settled decipherment.
On certainty
Every marker on this plate carries an interpretation, not a caption the codex supplies for itself. The Codex Borgia has no glosses; its meaning is reconstructed from comparative iconography, colonial texts, and — in Milbrath's case — retrocalculated astronomy. The eclipse identification in particular is a proposal, not an established fact. Treat the identifications here as a well-argued reading that other specialists dispute.
