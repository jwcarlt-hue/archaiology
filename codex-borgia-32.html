Cosmogenesis and the New Year

Plate 32 opens a new year — 4 Flint — and with it the veintena of Cuauhuitlehua, honoring the rain gods, the Tlaloque. At the centre, Susan Milbrath reads an image of cosmogenesis: the birth of the planetary gods, among them four Tezcatlipocas and the first anthropomorphic Quetzalcoatl, shown as an infant Venus god. At the foot of the page, the same bearded Quetzalcoatl passes through a starry Milky Way goddess — marked, Milbrath suggests, by a comet sighted in February 1496. Tap a marker to explore the imagery.

Provenance
The Codex Borgia is a Central Mexican divinatory manuscript. Painted before the Spanish conquest in the Puebla-Tlaxcala Valley — most likely by Tlaxcaltec or Cholultec scribes of the "Mixteca-Puebla" tradition — its imagery is Nahua and its gods Aztec.

Features on the plate

Reading the page

After Milbrath 1989, 2013 · synopsis after Byland 1993
A new year begins
Plate 31 closed the old year in its double frame of Izcalli and the nemontemi; plate 32 opens the next, dated by Milbrath to 20 February – 10 March 1496 (Julian), the start of the year 4 Flint. The veintena is Cuauhuitlehua — also called Xilomanaliztli or Atlcahualo — which honored the Tlaloque, the rain gods, shown here in a nocturnal frame with knives.
Cosmogenesis
The central image, Milbrath suggests, depicts the birth of the planetary gods — a cosmogenesis celebrated in an annual ritual. Four Tezcatlipocas, each born with a smoking mirror on his brow, reappear elsewhere on the page with the mirror relocated to one foot; among Itztli's five offspring is the first anthropomorphic image of Quetzalcoatl in the manuscript, shown as an infant Venus god.
A comet in February 1496
At the base of the page, a bearded Quetzalcoatl passes through the starry body of a Milky Way goddess — the same Venus-through-the-Milky-Way motif seen on plates 29–31. Milbrath proposes that the "smoking eye" on this Quetzalcoatl figure may record a real comet, first sighted in February of 1496.
Itztli, the knife moon
A lunar deity, Itztli ("Obsidian Knife"), appears near the top of the page and may represent the moon in its waxing or waning quarter — a "knife moon" whose half-lit disk resembles a blade. Full moon fell on 28 February 1496, within this veintena.
On certainty
Every marker on this plate carries an interpretation, not a caption the codex supplies for itself. The Codex Borgia has no glosses; its meaning is reconstructed from comparative iconography, colonial texts, and — in Milbrath's case — retrocalculated astronomy. The comet and lunar-phase identifications in particular are proposals, not established facts. Treat the identifications here as a well-argued reading that other specialists dispute.
