Solar Alignments
Temples, mounds, and megalithic monuments worldwide were oriented to capture solstice or equinox sunrises. From Stonehenge to Karnak to Cahokia's woodhenges, solar alignment was a fundamental architectural practice across unconnected cultures.
⭐
Stellar Calendars
The heliacal rising of the Pleiades marked the new year across Mesopotamia, Greece, and the Andes. Sirius rising heralded the Nile flood for ancient Egyptians. The Lidar Höyük prism may track such stellar horizon events as a portable parapegma.
🌙
Lunar Standstills
The 18.6-year lunar standstill cycle — the Moon's maximum and minimum rising points — was tracked at Callanish, the Newark Earthworks octagon, and potentially at Chaco Canyon's Chimney Rock great house. The Newark Octagon is precisely aligned to this cycle.

Maya Astronomy · Xultun
Sak Tahn Waax
Maya astronomer · Xultun · 8th century CE

Named in the painted numerals of Structure 10K-2 at Xultun, Guatemala — a scribal workroom whose walls carry lunar tables and long-interval calculations running far beyond a single human lifetime. The Xultun texts show working astronomers refining the fit between the 260-day count, the solar year, and the cycles of the Moon and Venus.

Greek & Roman Astronomy · Miletus
Anaximander of Miletus
c. 610 – c. 546 BCE

The Milesian who first proposed that the Earth hangs unsupported at the centre of things, held in place by nothing but symmetry. He is credited in the doxographic tradition with the gnomon, a map of the inhabited world, and a model of the heavens built from proportion rather than myth — the earliest surviving attempt to give the cosmos a geometry.

How it works
Romans counted days inclusively backward from three fixed points: Kalendae (1st), Nonae (5th or 7th), and Idus (13th or 15th, depending on month). A date is expressed as "the Nth day before" the next of these — e.g. June 21st falls "ante diem XI Kalendas Iulias."
Chris Bennett · Ptolemaic Chronology · Tyndale House

Republican Conversion Table

The calculator above assumes the Julian calendar. Before Caesar's reform of 46 BC the Roman civil year was a 355-day lunar-ish year kept in rough step with the sun by intercalation at the discretion of the pontifex maximus — so a Republican date maps onto no fixed Julian day without reconstruction. This is Chris Bennett's reconstruction: for every civil year from 263 BC to AD 60 it gives the Julian date on which each Roman month began.

Reading a row
Each row is one Roman civil year. Year is the Julian year in which it began (it may end in the next). AUC is the Varronian year ab urbe condita, counting AUC 1 = 753 BC. Each month cell gives the Julian date of that month's Kalends — its first day. Click any row to expand it.
Intercalaris
The intercalary month, inserted after the Terminalia in late Februarius to hold the calendar against the sun. A date here means the year was intercalary (377 or 378 days). Caesar abolished it in 46 BC; that year's two replacement months appear as Int I and Int II, giving the famous 445-day annus confusionis.
Δ = ±N cells
Between AUC 492 = 262 BC and AUC 564 = 190 BC the number of intercalations between two years is often recoverable while their individual lengths are not. Bennett records the resulting slack in the Intercalaris cell as Δ = +N/−M — the maximum days the true date may fall after or before the one shown. These cells are not intercalations.
Nundinal letters
The letter A–H of the market day in the eight-day nundinal cycle — one of the strongest checks on any reconstruction. A pair marks an intercalary year (before and after insertion). Note Bennett's letters are the mod-8 complement of the usual modern convention:
Roman      A B C D E F G H
Conventional A H G F E D C B
Month names
Republican names are used throughout. Quintilis became Iulius in 44 BC; Sextilis became Augustus in 8 BC; September was briefly Germanicus under Caligula, AD 37–40.

Near Eastern Astronomy · Babylon
Berossus
Early 3rd century BCE

Priest of Marduk at Babylon and author of the Babyloniaca, written in Greek for a Hellenistic readership. Berossus stands at the hinge where the cuneiform astronomical tradition — the MUL.APIN star lists, the Diaries, the mathematical lunar theory — passed into the Greek world. Later writers made him a teacher of astronomy on Kos; how much is biography and how much legend remains unsettled.

MUL.APIN · Tablet I · Hunger & Steele 2018 · Taylor & Francis

Babylonian Star Catalogue

The three star paths of MUL.APIN — the most widely copied astronomical text in ancient Mesopotamia, composed before the 8th century BCE. 71 stars and constellations assigned to the paths of Enlil (north, >+17° dec.), Anu (equatorial, ±17°), and Ea (south, <−17°), with their Babylonian names, associated deities, heliacal rising months, and modern identifications. Babylonian Conversion Table

The Babylonian civil year was lunar: twelve or thirteen months, each beginning with the evening first sighting of the new crescent, kept in step with the seasons by intercalating a thirteenth month. Converting a Babylonian or Seleucid date to the Julian calendar therefore means knowing exactly when each month began — recoverable, for this period, from the astronomical diaries, lunar tables and eclipse records. This is Bennett's corrected table: Parker & Dubberstein's 1956 reconstruction overlaid with the contemporary cuneiform evidence. For every year from 331 to 30 BC it gives the Julian date on which each month began. Because the Babylonian day began at sunset, each month actually opened on the evening before the Julian date shown.

Reading a row
Each row is one Babylonian civil year, beginning in the spring with Nisanu. The Julian year is the year BC in which that Nisanu fell. Each month cell gives the Julian date of its first day; click a row to see the full year with month lengths and the Macedonian equivalents.
Seleucid Era · SEB & SEM
The era was inaugurated in SE 7 = 305/4 BC, reckoned retroactively to SE 1 = 311/0; those notional early years show in parentheses. The Babylonian count SEB begins each year at Nisanu; the Macedonian count SEM is one higher between Tashritu and the next Nisanu. Toggle which you see above.
Before the era
Years before SE 7 are dated by the regnal years of Alexander III, Philip III and Alexander IV. The situation in these years is genuinely tangled — rival reckonings by Antigonus and Seleucus overlap — and Bennett's regnal column reflects one resolution of it.
Intercalation
A thirteenth month kept the lunar year aligned with the seasons: a second Ululu (Ululu II) at mid-year, or far more often a second Addaru (Addaru II) at year's end. Intercalary years run thirteen months; they are highlighted and filterable above.
Month lengths
Every length shown is recomputed here from the interval between consecutive Julian month-starts, so each is a clean 29 or 30 days. This corrects three cells in the source spreadsheet whose stored lengths (one showing 28) were artefacts of how BC dates were held in Excel across a February boundary — the month dates were always right; three lengths were not.

Egyptian Astronomy · Thebes
Senenmut
Steward of Amun under Hatshepsut · c. 1470 BCE

Overseer of works for Hatshepsut and the man whose unfinished tomb at Deir el-Bahari (TT353) carries the earliest surviving Egyptian astronomical ceiling. Its northern panel sets out the circumpolar constellations and the Bull's Foreleg; its southern panel lays out the decanal belt whose heliacal risings divided the night into hours.

Egyptian Archaeoastronomy

The Decan Catalogue

The 36 decans are groups of stars used by ancient Egyptians to divide the sky into 10-degree segments, each rising heliacally every ten days. Together they mark the 360-day Egyptian civil year (plus five epagomenal days). Used as a sidereal star clock from at least the 9th Dynasty (c.2100 BCE), each decan governed one hour of the night sky and carried a corresponding deity.

Scholarly caveat (Sarah Symons, AEA McMaster): "Although we know the names of the decans, and in some cases can translate the names, the locations of the decanal stars and their relationships to modern star names are not known." Stellar identifications below are marked CONFIRMED (broad consensus), PROBABLE (strong argument, contested), or UNCERTAIN (proposed only). The firmament above Thebes

The astronomical ceiling in Senenmut's tomb at Deir el-Bahari brings the decanal tradition into a painted cosmic map. Following Jose Lull and Juan Antonio Belmonte's A Firmament Above Thebes, the interactive version separates the southern decans from the northern constellation field, marking secure identifications such as Sopdet/Sirius, Sah/Orion, and the Bull's Foreleg while keeping probable and disputed readings visible.
