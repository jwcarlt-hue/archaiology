Interactive Plate · Codex Borgia 30

The Rising of the Morning Star

Plate 30 is the second page of the Codex Borgia's Venus almanac and the counterpart to plate 29. Where 29 showed the Evening Star dying in the fire, 30 shows its rebirth: Venus reappears in the east as the brilliant Morning Star, painted as a resplendent rayed disk. On Susan Milbrath's reading the page marks the heliacal rise of the Morning Star — M-first — within the festival of Tititl. Tap a marker to explore the imagery.

Provenance
The Codex Borgia is a Central Mexican divinatory manuscript. Painted before the Spanish conquest in the Puebla-Tlaxcala Valley — most likely by Tlaxcaltec or Cholultec scribes of the "Mixteca-Puebla" tradition — its imagery is Nahua and its gods Aztec.

Reading the page

After Milbrath 1989, 2013 · synopsis after Byland 1993
The heliacal rise
Where plate 29 ended in the underworld, plate 30 brings Venus back. Milbrath reads the rayed disk as the heliacal rise of the Morning Star (M-first), which retrocalculation — Aveni and Bricker independently — places on 12 January 1496 (Julian), midway through the dry season.
The festival of Tititl
The page corresponds to the veintena of Tititl, the seventeenth twenty-day festival, second from the end of the Aztec and Tlaxcalan year (per the Relaciones). Milbrath's argument is that the eighteen festivals of the year coordinate, page by page, with the actual events of the Venus cycle.
A calendar you can turn
The twenty day-signs ringing the disk run counterclockwise from Crocodile through Flower. Circled signs, Milbrath suggests, let the almanac be used to calculate festival dates across a four-year span — the page working as an instrument, not only an image.
Quetzalcoatl's rayed headdress
Elsewhere in the Borgia (pages 9 and 19) Quetzalcoatl wears a segment of the Venus disk — red and black rays on a star ground — as his headdress. Here that segment appears in full as the complete round disk: the insignia and the planet are one and the same.
On certainty
Every marker on this plate carries an interpretation, not a caption the codex supplies for itself. The Codex Borgia has no glosses; its meaning is reconstructed from comparative iconography, colonial texts, and — in Milbrath's case — retrocalculated astronomy. Other specialists (Seler, Nowotny, Anders & Jansen, Boone) read these pages differently. Treat the identifications here as a well-argued proposal, not a settled decipherment.
