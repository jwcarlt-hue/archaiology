Xipe's Sacrifice at the Spring Equinox

Plate 33 is the best-attested festival scene in the whole almanac: multiple independent studies identify its temple imagery as Tlacaxipehualiztli, the spring-equinox festival of Xipe Totec. The sun disk crowning the temple's peak marks a real observation — the sun rising behind the pyramid on the equinox — while inside, a black Quetzalcoatl bestows power on Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, the Morning Star, at the moment of Venus's highest altitude. Provenance
The Codex Borgia is a Central Mexican divinatory manuscript. Painted before the Spanish conquest in the Puebla-Tlaxcala Valley — most likely by Tlaxcaltec or Cholultec scribes of the "Mixteca-Puebla" tradition — its imagery is Nahua and its gods Aztec.

A temple, an equinox, a sacrifice.

A great yellow pyramid dominates the page: the sun crowns its peak, Quetzalcoatl and the Morning Star god occupy its steps, and Mercury hides invisibly behind it. Around the temple, the sky carries the ecliptic as a twisted cord, Jupiter as a knife-helmeted face, and — at the base — Xipe Totec's own sacrifice. Reading the page

After Milbrath 1989, 2013 · synopsis after Byland 1993
The best-attested page in the sequence
Where earlier plates rest on Milbrath's reconstruction alone, page 33 is independently confirmed by multiple scholars as Tlacaxipehualiztli, the festival of Xipe Totec. In colonial-period codices Xipe appears exclusively in scenes of this festival — so his sacrifice here is not a proposed identification but a documented one. Milbrath dates the page to 11–30 March 1496 (Julian), overlapping the spring equinox on 12 March.
A real sunrise observation
The sun disk crowning the temple's roof-comb is read as a record of actual practice: Aztec priests observed the sun rising behind a specific temple to mark the equinox, a method described by the friar Motolinía. The image may depict this alignment directly — the sun "standing" over the pyramid on the day the year turned.
Venus at its highest
Inside the temple, black Quetzalcoatl (Venus) gives his power to Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, the Morning Star's own god — a scene Milbrath connects to Venus reaching its maximum altitude on 27 February 1496, shortly before this page's date range. Xolotl, Quetzalcoatl's twin and a figure for Mercury, hides behind the temple — invisible, as Mercury is near conjunction.
A dry-season sacrifice
The Venus gods are shown sacrificing Tlaloc, the rain god — fitting, Milbrath notes, for a dry time of year. Xipe's own death on the temalacatl, the round stone of gladiatorial combat, closes the reading: two sacrifices, of the rain god and of Xipe, both proper to the last weeks before the rains return.
On certainty
The Xipe/Tlacaxipehualiztli identification on this page is unusually well supported — confirmed independently by Nowotny, Milbrath, and others, and consistent with how Xipe is depicted throughout colonial-period sources. The finer identifications — Jupiter as a knife-helmeted face, Xolotl's exact position, the precise Venus-altitude date — remain Milbrath's own interpretation, reconstructed from comparative iconography and retrocalculated astronomy, and are not confirmed the way Xipe's festival is.
